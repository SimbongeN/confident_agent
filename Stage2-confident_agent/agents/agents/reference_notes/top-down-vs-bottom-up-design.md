# Top-down vs bottom-up design
keywords: top-down, bottom-up, stepwise refinement, structured design, structured programming, stubs, decomposition, Harlan Mills, Wirth
- Top-down design starts with a complex system and divides it into successively smaller pieces; also called stepwise design or stepwise refinement.
- In top-down design a main procedure names the major functions it needs, and each is refined until simple enough to code.
- Bottom-up design specifies base elements in detail first, then links them into larger subsystems until the complete system exists.
- Top-down emphasizes planning and full understanding of the system; coding waits until the design is detailed enough.
- In top-down design, testing of functional units is delayed until significant design is complete, and stubs stand in for modules.
- Bottom-up emphasizes coding and early testing, which can start once the first module is specified.
- Bottom-up risk: modules may be written without a clear idea of how they connect, and linking them may be harder than expected.
- Bottom-up relies on existing modules and object-oriented programming; reusability of code is one of its main benefits.
- Harlan Mills at IBM developed structured programming concepts and tested them in a 1969 New York Times project, spreading top-down design.
- Top-down methods were favored until the late 1980s; modern software design usually combines both approaches.
